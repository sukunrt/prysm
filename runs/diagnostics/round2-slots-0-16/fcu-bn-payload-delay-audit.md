# FCU response to beacon-node payload-ID marker

## Result

The execution layer was fast in every owner's successful payload-preparing
`engine_forkchoiceUpdatedV4` call, but the matching beacon-node marker was
usually late before the first successful proposals. Of the 15 owner slots with
a comparable marker, the proxy-response-to-marker median is **1,275.320 ms**;
12/15 exceed 300 ms and 9/15 exceed one second. Restricting the comparison to
failed slots 2--14 gives a **1,482.789 ms** median, 12/13 over 300 ms, and a
5,668.310 ms maximum. The first successful proposers are different: node 32 at
slot 15 takes **1.781 ms**, and node 76 at slot 16 takes **0.389 ms**.

This is stronger evidence for broad, intermittent beacon-node servicing or
lock pressure during startup than for slow execution-layer payload work. It is
also cross-method evidence: the matching `engine_getPayloadV6` calls return
HTTP 200 through the proxy in 0--22 ms, while four failed builds do not report
the result until 308.064--1,869.824 ms after the proxy response. The special
300 ms GetPayload context explains the terminal error path in slots 5, 6, 8,
and 9, but it cannot explain seconds-long FCU visibility gaps in preflight,
parent-state, and consensus-branch owners.

The exact internal queue remains unresolved. The FCU marker is downstream of
more work than the JSON-RPC call, and the snooper marker is not a packet or BN
decoder-completion timestamp. The supported hypothesis is therefore shared
pressure in the broad post-proxy/BN servicing envelope, potentially expressed
through different locks and runnable delays in different callers. It is not a
measurement of one shared mutex or one scheduler queue.

## Exact owner/slot/ID join

`fcu_bn_payload_delay.tsv` is generated by:

```text
python3 runs/diagnostics/round2-slots-0-16/analyze_fcu_bn_payload_delay.py
```

The engine timeline retains an eight-byte payload ID. Prysm logs the exact
first six bytes through `bytesutil.Trunc`, so the script requires the same
owner node, `target_slot == nextSlot`, and exact 12-hex-digit ID prefix. It then
selects the unique matching response preceding the BN marker. All 15 measured
slots have exactly one such response.

Node 169 has a VALID slot-1 FCU response and payload ID at
`01:30:12.167372899Z`, but its complete beacon log has no matching payload-ID
message. This is **not** a right-censored delay. At the historical source
revision the Gloas `fcuFromReorgData` path caches and publishes the returned ID
without emitting this message, while `lateBlockTasks` returns immediately when
the current slot equals the head slot. The caller is not identified in the
retained records, so slot 1 has no comparable endpoint.

Two owners have nearby repeated events. Node 191 receives the same full slot-2
ID again 19.468 seconds after its BN marker. Node 76 receives the same full
slot-16 ID again 9.376 seconds after its marker and also receives another
slot-16 ID. The script retains these counts and proxy IDs in the TSV. They do
not make the selected joins ambiguous because each marker has exactly one
preceding same-node, same-slot, exact-prefix response.

## Cross-method comparison

The old node-118 and node-83 examples generalize. Their FCU proxy calls finish
in 2 and 24 ms, followed by matching BN markers **5,668.310 ms** and **756.305
ms** later. Nodes 19 and 107 add 670.297 ms and 1,588.719 ms cases. All four
later issue same-ID GetPayload calls whose successful proxy responses take
0--22 ms; the BN then reports its mapped timeout 1,869.824, 308.064, 810.412,
and 1,507.219 ms after those responses, respectively.

The GetPayload response is consumed promptly once the system recovers enough
to build. For packing-path owners 35 and 85, the proxy response precedes
`Chose payload bid source=self-build` by **24.204 ms** and **2.434 ms**. For the
first successes, the same interval is **3.297 ms** on node 32 and **6.299 ms**
on node 76. Their FCU response-to-marker gaps are also the two smallest in the
table. Thus a particular payload ID or slow geth construction does not explain
the earlier gaps. The historical 0--22 ms proxy timings establish fast EL and
proxy-side response production, while the later BN observation varies widely.

The chronology does not show smooth recovery across slots 2--14. Their delays
remain irregular, including 4.266 seconds at slot 10, 33.964 ms at slot 11,
4.257 seconds at slot 13, and 2.418 seconds at slot 14; Spearman rank
correlation between slot and delay is exactly 0.000 for these 13 points. The
sub-2 ms values at slots 15 and 16 are consistent with a late recovery, and
both owners then build successfully. Two outcome-selected success observations
cannot establish a recovery threshold or prove that the FCU gap determined
proposal success.

## Relation to the named failures

Node 91's DomainData-preflight failure coexists with a **1,230.209 ms** FCU
post-response gap, so the delayed-service pattern is present on one of the two
DomainData owners. Node 169 has no comparable FCU log endpoint. FCU runs in a
different goroutine from validator DomainData, and neither archive has server
admission spans; these observations do not assign the DomainData latency to
the same queue or lock.

Packing-path owners 35 and 85 have large earlier FCU gaps of **4,266.405 ms**
and **2,418.308 ms**, but their later same-ID GetPayload responses are consumed
quickly and self-build payloads are selected. Their required consensus branches
then remain unfinished. This supports transient shared pressure as background
context, while leaving the historical packing inputs and internal phase times
unobserved. It does not turn the FCU delay into the cause of either packing
failure.

## Source boundary and timestamp limits

At revision `0280403c70d88967f49d2d4c730f4c5417dabdf5`, the measured Gloas
message is emitted by `lateBlockTasks` after `notifyForkchoiceUpdateGloas`
returns, after `PayloadIDCache.Set` takes its mutex, and after `HeadSlot` takes
the head read lock while constructing the log fields. Before returning,
`ForkchoiceUpdated` still has to receive and decode the JSON-RPC response and
validate its payload-status fields. Consequently the measured interval can
include proxy flush/delivery, client read and decode, goroutine scheduling,
the payload-ID cache mutex, the head lock, and logging.

The snooper records its response after synchronous upstream handling and
`io.Copy` into the downstream response abstraction, but it does not explicitly
flush. Its log and the BN/container log are asynchronous observations on
different processes. The interval is a reproducible outer-timestamp ordering,
not a network transit measurement. Exact IDs establish event identity; they do
not remove those observation limits.
